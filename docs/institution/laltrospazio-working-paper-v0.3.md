## A note on sources, before anything else

This paper draws on four bodies of evidence, each with a different authority and a different blind spot:

1. **The interview corpus** (S01–S12 in Appendix A) — testimony from early workers and the founders, gathered as oral history. Strong on lived experience; weak on dates and legal fact.
2. **Formal and administrative records** (S13–S30, C001–C050) — financial statements, board minutes, public-administration agreements, filings. Strong on point-in-time fact; silent on almost everything about lived experience, and occasionally internally inconsistent with itself (a tax number recorded wrong, a capital field that disagrees with itself across two documents in the same year).
3. **The Facebook archive**, 2014–2025 (C-FB-01–C-FB-29) — 930 public posts, 200 thin event records, and seven photo albums, systematically reconstructed for this version of the paper. Strong on contemporaneous public self-presentation and on dating public events precisely; structurally silent on governance, ownership and anything the organisation did not choose to post about.
4. **Owner-origin narrative material** (C-ORIG-01–C-ORIG-13) — three overlapping literary treatments of the same founders' memory, prepared for eventual public storytelling. Strong on motivation, feeling and design intent; explicitly not independent corroboration of itself no matter how many times a detail is repeated across the three versions, and not authoritative on dates or entities.

The working rule across all four is the one this project has used from the start: **a source's authority and a claim's accuracy are separate questions.** A signed public-administration pact can still misstate a tax number. A single Facebook post can still be the best surviving record of an exact date. A founder's memory of a bell ringing at a notary's office is real and worth keeping, and it is not a legal fact.

Where a claim in this paper rests on a specific piece of evidence, the relevant identifier appears in parentheses. C-FB-numbers and C-ORIG-numbers resolve fully inside this repository, in `docs/institution/research/facebook/facebook-historical-evidence-register.md` and `docs/institution/research/origins-source-note.md` respectively. S-numbers, O01 and C0-numbers originate in Working Paper v0.2's own source appendix and claim register, which remain outside this repository (owner-held); the specific identifiers this paper actually relies on are catalogued, in sanitised form, in `docs/institution/research/v0.2-source-evidence-index.md` — enough to check what each citation means and what it does and does not establish, without importing the underlying off-repo documents. Readers who just want the story can skip all of this.

This paper does not resolve every date, and it should not try to. The evidence-extraction phase behind it is deliberately closed for this version: what follows is what more than a decade of overlapping, sometimes contradictory record-keeping actually supports, not what a tidier organisation might wish it supported.

## 1. What this paper is

Working Paper v0.1 was a conceptual synthesis: interviews, commentary, and a set of seven working propositions about how exclusion and inclusion actually work. Working Paper v0.2 added an institutional layer — company records, board minutes, a public-administration pact — and discovered that L'Altro Spazio's legal history is considerably more complicated, and more interesting, than "a cooperative since 2010."

This version adds a third layer: roughly a decade of the organisation's own public voice, reconstructed from its Facebook archive, plus first-person origin narratives from both founders. Together, these do three things v0.2 could not do.

First, they push the story back before 2015 and give it a real predecessor: a venue called Via Polese, whose abrupt closure after serious water damage directly caused the venue that followed. Second, they show the practice was never confined to one address — it moved through at least five physical environments under four distinct L'Altro Spazio legal entities, plus Associazione Farm's separate and overlapping presence at Parco, including one workspace concept that never became a going concern. Third, they let the organisation's own words be read against its own record: how it talked about disability and accessibility in 2016 is not how it talks now, and neither version is simply "wrong" — they are evidence of a practice that sometimes moved faster than its own vocabulary, and a vocabulary that later sometimes claimed more than any one activity could show.

This paper is still not a finished history. It is a working synthesis, built to be corrected. It exists to let the organisation, and eventually its public communication, tell a true and specific story instead of a generic one. Two owner-approved working interpretations frame the synthesis and are used explicitly, and labelled as interpretations, throughout: that L'Altro Spazio may be better understood as a *practice* of creating and reconfiguring social, cultural and hospitality environments than as a single venue; and that two founders' distinct starting experiences — a route through **gaze and visibility**, and a route through **translation and mediation** — converged into a shared practice concerned with space, structure and participation. Neither interpretation is asked to carry more weight than the evidence gives it.

## 2. Before L'Altro Spazio

The name "L'Altro Spazio" is usually associated with 2015. The practice behind it starts earlier, and starts with someone noticing something, not building something.

Around 2010, Nunzia Vannuccini was working on an urban-regeneration project at Parco 11 Settembre in Bologna. She noticed a group of Deaf people signing to one another — "it looked like a dance," in her own later description — and, self-taught and informal, began spending time with Bologna's Deaf community (C-ORIG-01). This is not a retrospectively tidy origin story: Nunzia's own words describe falling "in love with the language before I knew its rules," which is a description of curiosity, not of a plan.

The date matters and is unusually well supported for an origin story: it rests on the owner's own account (O01), on a first-person Facebook post Nunzia wrote in November 2020 — six years before any interview for this project was conducted, tied to a municipal participatory-budget campaign, stating she met her first Deaf people "nel 2010" and that Italian Sign Language (LIS) is now "la prima lingua di mia figlia," her own daughter's first language (C-FB-15) — and on the origin narrative's own account of the same period (C-ORIG-01). Three genuinely different moments of record-keeping, six years apart at minimum, converge on the same year without having been assembled for that purpose.

What Nunzia describes next is the conceptual hinge of the whole institution. Through her friendships with Deaf people she came to see what she calls "invisible barriers" separating people from social, cultural and professional life — and the moment that crystallised this into a problem worth building something around was mundane: a customer's remark, during the first months of an early project, that they had "never imagined Deaf people were normal people" (C-ORIG-02, C-ORIG-03). Her own formulation of what that remark revealed has stayed remarkably stable over the decade since: *"Non mancavano loro. Erano i nostri spazi a essere stati costruiti senza pensarle"* — "They were not missing. Our spaces had simply been built without them in mind." From that point she generalised the same logic to blind people, wheelchair users and people with intellectual disabilities: not "missing," but un-imagined by the environments other people had already built (C-ORIG-04).

This is Nunzia's route into the practice, and it is worth naming precisely because it is not the same route Jascha took. Hers begins with **gaze and visibility** — she does not initially experience exclusion directly; she witnesses it, and is struck by how thoroughly ordinary social imagination fails to include people it has simply never pictured. The other founder's route starts from the inside of exactly that failure.

Parco 11 Settembre itself deserves to be read as more than a backdrop. Across the whole Facebook archive it is the single most continuously evidenced thread in the organisation's history — an unbroken seasonal rhythm, confirmed every year the archive covers, running under Associazione Farm's cultural programme "Cineporto BOff" from at least the mid-2010s onward, later drawing in a 2020 municipal participatory-budget campaign, a 2023 Comune "Diversity Team" event, and by 2024 an explicit cooperative-credited children's programme, "Il Porto Verde di Bologna," co-delivered with Associazione Farm and three other partners (C-FB-05, C-FB-12, C-FB-16, C-FB-17, C-FB-24; §8 below). Long before any legal entity called itself L'Altro Spazio, this park was already the site where culture, public space and disability-community life were being brought into the same place.

## 3. Translation and the edge of participation

Jascha Blume's route into the same eventual practice runs through a different experience entirely, and this paper deliberately keeps it distinct rather than folding it into Nunzia's story or into one generic "founding narrative."

At the Rietveld Academy in Amsterdam, Jascha describes a "difficult period" of never quite receiving full confirmation of what was being said around him, always a beat behind conversations mediated through interpreters — "close to the conversation, but never completely inside it" (C-ORIG-06). He describes living between two worlds, Deaf friends and the hearing art world, belonging fully to neither: "always translating between different worlds, without being completely inside one." After graduating, an isolated, at times depressed period followed, working alone in a shared studio container at Amsterdam's NDSM/Kunststad site, exploring "the tension between real and unreal, virtual and physical," and inviting Deaf friends there partly, in his own words, "not to be alone" (C-ORIG-07).

This is not an anecdote about disability in the abstract; it is a specific, repeatable experience of structural distance — being routed through someone else's translation, never quite arriving inside the room. It is worth stating plainly because it is the single strongest piece of evidence, in any source reviewed for this paper, for what becomes Proposition 2: that translation is not something that happens after meaning is made, but one of the ways meaning is made in the first place.

Nunzia invited Jascha to Bologna after seeing his documentary "I Sign, I Live" at a film festival — by her account, in Rome — initially, in her own words, as "an artistic invitation"; "only later did we understand that this encounter would open much more" (C-ORIG-05). Jascha accepted, flew to Bologna, and describes being "overwhelmed" — by Nunzia's curiosity and intuition toward sign language, and by Italy generally (C-ORIG-08). The Rome screening itself is a minor, non-blocking gap in the record: no Rome festival appears anywhere in the 2014 Facebook material, though a separate, clearly documented screening of the same film took place at Bologna's Cineporto BOff on 7 June 2014 (C-FB-01). Both can be true — a Rome screening prompting the invitation, and a later Bologna screening as a distinct public event — and this paper does not need to resolve which came first to use the story.

What Jascha describes from that first Bologna summer is the detail that most directly foreshadows the institution to come. Working behind the bar at the Parco 11 Settembre festival, he found himself "astonished" that communication was easier than he expected: customers took their time, used gesture, "were not afraid of hands" — giving him, in his words, "a strange comfort," and prompting an explicit comparison: "in the Netherlands it probably would not be like this" (C-ORIG-09). This is independently consistent with material gathered years later and from an entirely different source: a 2021 quote from the respected disability-affairs outlet Redattore Sociale describing children at the same park asking staff for water "in Lis" as an unselfconscious, ordinary thing to do (`programme-history.md` §4; part of the basis for C-FB-25). Two different people, six years apart, describing the same park as a place where sign language and gesture had simply become normal rather than remarkable.

## 4. Via Polese: the first L'Altro Spazio

Here the evidence base for this paper changes in an important way from v0.2, which did not know this section existed.

A venue called L'Altro Spazio operated at Via Polese 7 in Bologna from February to April 2014, run by an association distinct from Associazione Farm called **Associazione L'Altro Spazio**. Nunzia opened it shortly before Jascha's involvement began; Jascha was invited there specifically to present "I Sign, I Live," and this presentation — not a later, more institutional occasion — is where Jascha and Nunzia met in person for the first time (OWNER-CONFIRMED; C-FB-01). Maurice, one of the venue's early workers interviewed independently for this project's earlier phases, remembers Via Polese as the *"primissimo Altro Spazio"* — the very first L'Altro Spazio — without prompting from any of this later research (CORROBORATED TESTIMONY; C-FB-01).

The archive corroborates the outline in specific, dateable detail. The Facebook account itself was registered on 15 February 2014, the same day as its first post, "Aspettando L'Altro Spazio… Work in progress!"; the venue announced itself operational on 2 March; a Braille sensitisation course on 1 April is the earliest disability-specific programming found anywhere in the whole archive; and on 9 April, Swiss public broadcaster TV Svizzera published an independent article, *"Un locale per sordo-ciechi a Bologna"* — "A venue for deaf-blind people in Bologna" — external press corroboration, within the venue's first two months, that Via Polese was publicly framed around **deafblind** accessibility specifically, a broader and different frame from the Deaf/LIS emphasis that becomes more visible later (C-FB-01, `facebook-archive-chronology.md` 2014).

The venue's operating life was short: two closure posts on the same day in early April ("lavori in corso," then "problemi tecnici") already show operational fragility in the weeks before the closure that followed. Via Polese closed after **serious water damage**, and Associazione L'Altro Spazio ceased operating shortly afterward (OWNER-CONFIRMED). The account then falls silent for sixteen months — 27 April 2014 to 26 October 2015 — a gap this paper does not read as a mystery, unlike earlier drafts of this research: it is the closure and its aftermath, not an unexplained absence (C-FB-01, C-FB-02).

This closure is the direct cause of what came next. It was Via Polese's abrupt end — not an abstract decision to found a social enterprise — that led Jascha to propose rebuilding the underlying idea from scratch, together, at a new location, under a new and separate legal entity (OWNER-CONFIRMED). Read this way, Via Polese is not a footnote or an archival curiosity to be explained away. It is the organisation's first short-lived incarnation, its first major discontinuity, and the direct hinge on which everything that followed turns. Nothing in the evidence establishes that the underlying concept itself had failed, or that the venue was economically unsuccessful, before the water damage forced its closure — the record supports an abrupt, externally forced ending, not a verdict on whether the idea was working.

One correction is worth stating explicitly, because an earlier version of the origin narrative material could be misread otherwise. All three of the owner-origin narrative documents describe the post-Polese decision to rebuild in language like *"Da quelle conversazioni nacque L'Altro Spazio"* — "From those conversations, L'Altro Spazio was born" — without naming Via Polese at all. Read carelessly, this could suggest the name and the underlying idea began only after the closure. They did not: both the name and the underlying idea — a hospitality space built around Deaf presence and sign language, with Deaf staff as professionals rather than assisted guests — already existed at Via Polese in 2014. What was born from those conversations was the **Via Nazario incarnation** specifically, not the institution's first idea (C-ORIG-05 through C-ORIG-11, cross-referenced in `origins-source-note.md` §4). This paper states the correction plainly so it does not need restating in future versions.

A separate, minor thread surfaced during this research and is retained here only as background, deliberately not developed further: a marketing video dated by its filename to late March 2014 shows footage captioned at different points with both a "Senza Nome"/Via Belvedere reference and an "l'Altro spazio"/Via Polese reference. Per the owner's own clarification, "Senza Nome" and Associazione Farm belong to an earlier Bologna project and collaboration context that predates Via Nazario, involving overlapping people and projects — background to this history, not a fourth venue or a rebrand story requiring reconstruction (C-FB-29). No inference is drawn from it beyond what is visibly in the file.

## 5. Starting again

What followed Via Polese's closure was deliberate, not incidental. Jascha and Nunzia decided to rebuild the concept together, and did so through a new, separate, 50/50-owned company: **L'Altro Spazio S.r.l.**, incorporated on **24 April 2015** before notary Rossella Ruffini in Bologna (DOCUMENTED — the deed itself, cross-checked against the Bologna Chamber of Commerce's public registry extract; C-FB-26).

The deed is worth describing in some detail, because it is one of the strongest pieces of formal evidence anywhere in this research for how the organisation's founding logic operated at the level of its own paperwork, not only its later public programme. The deed is bilingual: its legally operative text is in **English** — recorded as "known to the parties and to the notary" — with an Italian translation appended below it by the notary herself. In identifying the two parties, the deed states plainly that Nunzia Vannuccini is an Italian citizen who knows Italian and English, and that Jascha Blume is a Dutch citizen who does **not** know Italian, knows English, and is completely without hearing but can read and write (DOCUMENTED; C-FB-26). The company was structured with a two-member board, Vannuccini as President and Blume as the second director, and 50/50 ownership between them — all independently confirmed by the Chamber of Commerce's own public registry extract dated 12 May 2015.

This is a genuinely unusual thing to find at the level of a company's own constitutional paperwork: a documented language-access and translation procedure, built directly into the notarial procedure that created the company itself, following directly from the fact that one founder could not read Italian. Jascha's deafness is separately and independently recorded in the same deed; it is relevant context for the communication around the signing, but the deed's bilingual structure follows from the language barrier, not from deafness as such, and this paper is careful not to fold the two together into a single accommodation. It is worth resisting the temptation to over-dramatise the episode — a bilingual deed with a translation appended is not, by itself, an extraordinary legal document. What is notable is the coincidence between this documented translation procedure and the organisation's later, explicit theory that translation is not an afterthought but a mode of meaning-making (Proposition 2, §14 below). This is not offered as proof that the founders consciously designed their own incorporation as a demonstration of the proposition — there is no evidence they did — but as a real, documented instance of the same underlying logic operating at the organisation's origin, independent of anyone's intention.

The founders' own memory of the signing is personal and should be kept distinct from the deed itself. Nunzia recalls church bells ringing at the exact moment she signed, and "goosebumps"; the moment was filmed; the two went for a celebratory drink afterward. Jascha's own description: "It felt almost like a marriage" (ATTRIBUTED/OWNER MEMORY; C-FB-26). This is genuine and worth preserving as oral history. It is not documentary evidence, and a legal deed neither contains nor could contain it — the two should never be merged into a single "the deed shows this was like a marriage" sentence.

Six months later, on **29 October 2015**, Via Nazario Sauro L'Altro Spazio opened. This is one of the best-corroborated single dates in the whole institutional history: the archive shows a dense, real-time countdown across 26–29 October, announcing a 19:00 start, and culminating in an opening-night post — *"Stiamo aprendo! Alé!"* — published at 19:14 (C-FB-03). That timestamp establishes when the organisation publicly announced the doors were opening; it does not, by itself, establish the exact minute guests were physically let in, which is a different fact this paper does not claim to know. A matching second-anniversary post and photo album, both independently dated to late October/early November 2017, confirm the same opening date two years later without having been assembled for that purpose (C-FB-03).

One small case study in this paper's own method comes from comparing two different kinds of record of the same evening. The traditional origin narrative recalls the opening happening "an hour after planned," with guests already waiting outside while construction was still unfinished (C-ORIG-13). The Facebook archive shows a public post announcing the opening at 19:14, fourteen minutes after the advertised 19:00 start. These are not the same fact and should not be collapsed into one: the Facebook timestamp records when the organisation posted online that the doors were opening, not the moment guests actually walked in, which may reasonably have come later still. The owner's memory of a roughly hour-long final delay and the archive's 19:14 post can both be true at once — one describing a felt sense of how long the last push took, the other a specific public announcement — and this paper does not treat either as proof of the other.

## 6. Making Via Nazario

Via Nazario Sauro did not begin as a bar that was later made accessible. The construction narrative — drawn from the origin documents and independently reinforced by the interview corpus — describes a space that was designed as a social and cultural environment from an empty office onward, with hospitality as one component inside that design rather than its starting point.

The founders describe speaking to their landlord, and to each other, in terms of "activity, encounter, a social space" before "bar" language entered the conversation at all — language Jascha recalls "felt like the right language, because it was true" (C-ORIG-12). The landlord's own enthusiasm, on learning Nunzia was a teacher who wanted to involve "education, learning, and social life," is recorded as part of the same moment.

The design concept that followed was explicitly a "gallery with a bar" — a large white space, still visibly influenced by Jascha's time at Rietveld, in which the bar was "only one part of the activity" and exhibitions were intended to change over time. Material choices were made deliberately: birch and light wood for the bar structure and practical fittings, to "give stronger presence to the artworks"; white surfaces "because it felt clear, with less noise." Two specific visual works anchor this period — a thirty-sheet poster forming a SpaceX rocket-test image, and a hand-painted mural depicting "a signal, a deviation in frequency," which Jascha connects directly and personally to his own relationship with sound: "perhaps because sound never came easily to me" (`origins-source-note.md` §10, C-ORIG-13).

The building process itself was prototyped physically before it was built: tape marking out possible arrangements on floor and walls, hand-drawn plans and moodboards, and a period of reference-gathering — visiting other bars, flea markets, vintage shops — before construction proper began. The first build team combined trade specialists (an architect, a fast and skilled woodworker, a plumber-electrician) with people who would go on to work in the finished venue, many of them disabled — meaning at least some of the venue's future front-of-house staff were present during, and participating in, its physical construction, not simply hired afterward into a space someone else had finished (C-ORIG-13). The origin narrative names **Manuela, Michele, Chiara, Serena and others** as part of this early group. This is not three independent sources independently identifying the same four-person construction team — it is one narrative source naming them, alongside two separate, partial points of convergence: Manuela, Michele and Chiara independently appear as interview subjects connected to early work at the venue, and Serena separately appears in a 2017 Facebook post as the venue's own cook (matching Serena Sacco). Taken together this is a meaningful, mutually consistent picture of who was involved early on; it is not proof that all of them were present at construction specifically, and it is not proof of formal employment dates for any of them (`origins-source-note.md` §9).

It is worth being precise about what this evidence supports and what it does not. It supports a real, specific design intent, and a shared building process involving people who would later work in the venue. It does not, on its own, support a claim that every later accessibility feature — the Braille menus, the lowered counter, sign-language ordering, all independently documented three years later by Lonely Planet Italia's Bologna guide (`facebook-archive-chronology.md`, 22 Nov 2018) — was designed with the same explicit deliberateness as the aesthetic programme described here. The origin material is considerably more detailed about the art and aesthetic design intent than about the specific process by which accessibility features were decided. This is not a gap this paper tries to paper over.

The venue opened with construction still visibly incomplete, guests already waiting outside — a detail preserved above (§5) alongside the archive's own public 19:14 announcement post. Exactly how late the doors actually opened is not established by either source; what both agree on is that this was not a finished, polished launch, and that is the detail worth keeping.

## 7. Practice before theory

None of what follows in this paper's later sections about "propositions" and "institutionalisation" was articulated in 2015. The seven propositions used throughout this paper (§14) were formulated in 2026, as a way of reading back over eleven years of accumulated practice — not as a design brief the founders were working from at the time.

What the first years of Via Nazario actually show, across the interview corpus and the earliest Facebook material together, is something closer to improvisation under real operating pressure. Chiara describes a beer-tap mechanism being physically adapted until she could use it herself; Manuela describes a counter and service environment shaped around different bodies, developed through use rather than specified in advance; Michele describes forms of mutual assistance between colleagues that became routine rather than exceptional; Mauro describes relief, working here, from a demand he had felt elsewhere to conceal or overcompensate for visual impairment (S01–S06). None of these are accounts of a completed accessibility programme being applied. They are accounts of a workplace being adjusted, repeatedly, around the people actually in it.

The earliest recurring formats bear this out. Within weeks of opening, the venue had already established a weekly rhythm — a farm-to-table aperitivo, DJ nights, a Sunday brunch, a language-exchange evening — and by December 2015, less than two months after opening, the first "aperitivo al buio": sighted guests served and guided through the space by staff who were themselves blind or low-vision, not a novelty dining format but an inversion of who does the guiding (C-FB-23; see §8 for the format's full decade-long lifecycle). This is the direct ancestor of what the organisation now calls Cena al Buio, and its earliest form already carries the format's defining, decade-long feature: the service model itself, not just the guest experience, inverts who is assumed to see and who is assumed not to.

Associazione Farm was an active, publicly co-credited collaborator at Via Nazario from its very first months — a co-tagged film screening for the International Day Against Violence on Women in late November 2015 is the clearest instance — which complicates any attempt to draw a clean line between "Farm did Parco" and "the new SRL did Nazario" from day one (C-FB-04). The two threads of the organisation's practice were entangled with each other from the beginning, not sequentially separated as a simpler story might suggest.

Public and press attention arrived immediately and substantially: two segments on regional broadcaster TG3 Emilia-Romagna, a national Radio2 feature titled "Miracolo Italiano," a piece in the disability-affairs magazine SuperAbile titled "Quando l'accessibilità è di casa" ("when accessibility is at home"), and coverage from Il Resto del Carlino as a family-friendly venue listing — evidence that the venue was legible to outside observers, in multiple registers, within its first two months of operation (`facebook-archive-chronology.md` 2015). A radio interview that December is the archive's earliest recorded articulation adjacent to what becomes Proposition 1: Nunzia, on air, stating *"Le persone con disabilità non sono fatte di cristallo: sono, appunto, persone"* — "People with disabilities are not made of glass: they are, simply, people" — an explicit rejection of fragility framing, three months into the venue's existence, well before any of the later formal vocabulary existed to name what she meant.

## 8. Programmes as experiments

Rather than list every event the organisation has ever run, this section treats a handful of recurring programmes as case studies in how the practice actually developed — because the Facebook archive makes it possible, for the first time, to trace individual formats across a full decade rather than describing them only in the aggregate.

**Cena al Buio** is the clearest example of a format that persisted and consolidated without ever fundamentally changing its mechanism. It begins, under the name "aperitivo al buio," in December 2015; is already a recurring, TripAdvisor-corroborated format — with customer reviews quoted directly in the venue's own posts — by February 2016; briefly rebrands as "Prove al Buio" during the move to the new Pratello venue in December 2018; consolidates under its now-familiar name by November 2019; is promised at the third Pratello-area venue in 2022; disappears from the public record for the whole of 2024, for reasons the archive cannot explain (a promotional gap, not necessarily an operational one — see §11); and returns, fully branded and unchanged in concept, for Halloween 2025 — *"Spegni la luce, accendi i sensi,"* "turn off the light, turn on the senses" — a full decade after its origin (C-FB-23). Across ten years and at least three physical addresses, the format's core idea — that the *service*, not only the guest experience, inverts who does the seeing — never changed.

**Cineporto BOff and Parco 11 Settembre**, treated together, are the single most continuously evidenced thread in the whole archive — a seasonal summer rhythm confirmed in every year the archive covers with no genuine gap. What the archive adds to the picture is precision about *whose* programme this was and when: as late as September 2017, the park's summer season is still explicitly branded as Associazione Farm's "Cine-Porto BOff," with L'Altro Spazio credited only as a thematic collaborator on disability content — meaning the owner's own approximate dating of a "~2018" transition of Parco activity toward the SRL is not contradicted by this evidence, and is in fact narrowed by it, since a transition could not yet have happened publicly by late 2017 (C-FB-05). What the archive also shows, more surprisingly, is that Farm's public cultural co-branding of the park (the hashtag `#FARMAssociazioneCulturale`) persists in posts as late as 2023–2024, five or more years after any operational transfer is thought to have occurred (C-FB-06). This paper reads brand persistence and legal or operational control as two different, entirely compatible facts, not a contradiction requiring resolution — public branding, once established, does not necessarily track legal reality, in either direction.

The park's relationship with the Comune di Bologna also has a longer and more gradual history than a single 2025 agreement suggests. A November 2020 participatory-budget campaign — the same post in which Nunzia gives her personal LIS/2010 testimony — is an earlier, informal civic-engagement channel, four to five years before the formal 36-month collaboration pact described in §13 (C-FB-16). A November 2023 book presentation, co-hosted with the Comune's own internal "Diversity Team," is a second, distinct thread of engagement, this time disability-policy-specific rather than park-specific (C-FB-17). The 2025 pact did not appear from nothing; it formalised a relationship that had already been building, in more than one channel, for years.

**LIS and Deaf-community programming** is the clearest example in the whole archive of a practice that visibly *matures* rather than simply repeating. A recurring, seasonal LIS-awareness course, run with the partner organisation Ben Fatto - l'Altra scuola and aimed at educators and teachers, appears repeatedly across 2016–2017. Direct in-house LIS content is comparatively thin through 2018–2019 — a fluctuation in visible emphasis, not a discontinuation, since the thread returns strongly later. By 3 December 2023, the organisation convenes the richest formally-programmed disability-policy event found anywhere in the archive: a University of Bologna LIS professor presenting *Il Piccolo Principe* in sign language, a debate specifically on teaching LIS in primary schools, and a further debate on urban accessibility held jointly with the **Ente Nazionale Sordi**, Italy's national institution for Deaf people (C-FB-18). By April 2025, the same underlying thread reaches an internationally-funded artistic register: "The Sub_Bar Show," a touring installation exploring touch and subwoofer vibration as shared Deaf/hearing sensory material, and "Sensory Dialogues," a Deaf/hearing performance residency funded by the European Union and the Goethe-Institut through the "Culture Moves Europe" mobility programme (C-FB-25). Read end to end, this is a decade in which a language-awareness course became a university-and-national-institution policy convening and then internationally-funded contemporary art — not a flat repetition of the same idea, and not a story the organisation could have told about itself in 2016.

**Exhibitions, music and food** run continuously through nearly the entire archive and receive less individual attention in this paper only because their consistency, rather than their variation, is the notable fact. Visual art programming is close to monthly from November 2015 onward, including at least one explicit accessibility accommodation — Braille captions for a 2017 photography exhibition, produced with the Istituto dei ciechi Francesco Cavazza. Music runs at least weekly across almost the entire period, from early DJ residencies through "Spazi Migranti" (world-music nights built around migrant and refugee musicians, a flagship format by 2018 and still running in 2025) to an eleven-date curated baroque concert series in 2023. Food and hospitality follow the same pattern, culminating in "Aperitivo dal Mondo," a very-high-frequency, near-weekly world-food format explicitly run in collaboration with refugee and migrant associations and co-managed by people in situations of social and economic difficulty, rotating through dozens of countries between 2022 and 2025 (`programme-history.md` §5–6, 9).

**Employment and work-inclusion** language moves from a single systemic statement — "inclusion begins precisely with the right to work," May 2016 — to the archive's fullest policy statement, published in English in August 2018: an explicit distinction between legal-quota compliance under Italian law and the organisation's own stated choice to "hire people with a disability first, and not as a last resort" (`concept-language-evolution.md` §6). A specific, self-reported workforce figure — 80% of staff being people with disabilities — appears once, in October 2022, attached to the announcement of the venue's third location. This paper treats that figure exactly as the underlying research register does: a real, dated, self-reported claim, worth recording as evidence that the organisation was willing to quantify its own practice publicly, and not independently verifiable from anything found in this research. It should not be repeated as a verified statistic (C-FB-20).

Two smaller programme threads round out the picture of an organisation that treats accessibility as a design method applicable well beyond hospitality itself: a wheelchair-repair "ciclofficina," run from late 2016 with AUSL Bologna's mental-health and disability services; and RisiBO, a 2022 accessibility-mapping data project undertaken with UILDM Bologna, a national muscular-dystrophy rights organisation (`programme-history.md` §12).

## 9. Work, participation and agency

It would be easy, and wrong, to describe L'Altro Spazio's employment practice as a single sentence — "people with disabilities were employed here" — and move on. The interview corpus, read carefully, resists exactly that flattening.

Maurice frames disadvantage relationally: handicap, in his account, is not a property a person carries alone but something that appears in the relation between a person and an environment — a task, a counter, a rota, a piece of equipment. Chiara's account of the beer-tap mechanism is the same idea at its smallest, most concrete scale: change the mechanism, and a task that had required someone else's help no longer does. Manuela describes the bar counter itself as something reciprocally adapted through use, not delivered as a finished accessible fixture. Michele describes forms of mutual aid between colleagues becoming embedded in ordinary routine rather than treated as a special accommodation requiring acknowledgment each time. Mauro's account is explicitly comparative: relief, here, from a demand he had felt in other workplaces to conceal or compensate for visual impairment in order to be seen as competent. Daniel's testimony introduces a further, sharper dimension: an explicit concern with self-determination, and a criticism of hearing people's control over organisations that are supposed to serve Deaf communities — participation, in his account, is not the same thing as being spoken for (S01–S06).

What this collection of testimony supports, and what it does not, need to be kept separate. It supports a genuine, repeated pattern of adaptation, mutual aid, autonomy and ordinary professional responsibility — people learning tasks, solving problems, being relied upon, disagreeing, and building routines together, not simply being accommodated by an institution acting on them. It does not, by itself, establish formal employment dates, contract terms, or a verifiable current workforce composition for any specific period; nor does the fact that early team members participated in the physical construction of Via Nazario (§6) establish when their formal employment began, a distinction this paper is careful to preserve rather than blur into a single tidy narrative of "the people who built it were the people who worked there."

The organisation's own public language about work, traced across a decade, shows real continuity underneath a changing vocabulary: a 2016 statement that inclusion "begins precisely with the right to work"; a 2018 policy statement distinguishing legal compliance from organisational choice; and the 2022 workforce figure already discussed above and already flagged as unverified. What the public record conspicuously does *not* show, across the same decade, is the words "autonomy" and "participation" used as often or as explicitly as "work" and "labour inclusion" are — these ideas appear far more often in shared third-party content than in the organisation's own original framing (`concept-language-evolution.md` §6). This is a real gap between the seven propositions' emphasis on autonomy and participation as core ideas, and how consistently the organisation's own public voice has actually used those specific words. The underlying *practice*, as the interviews describe it, appears considerably more developed than the *vocabulary* the organisation has used, in public, to describe it — a pattern this paper returns to directly in §12.

## 10. More than one space

The interpretation offered at the top of this paper — that L'Altro Spazio may be better understood as a practice of creating and reconfiguring environments than as a single venue — is tested most directly here, against the fuller entity and venue picture this version of the paper can now support. It should be read as an interpretation throughout this section, not as a settled conclusion about legal identity.

By 2018, the organisation operating Via Nazario Sauro decided to open a second venue. **L'Altro Spazio Pratello SRLS** was incorporated on 25 May 2018 — a legal entity entirely separate from L'Altro Spazio S.r.l., though owned by the same two people in the same 50/50 split (OWNER-CONFIRMED; C-FB-07). Through this new entity, **L'Altro Spazio al Pratello**, at Via del Pratello 29/A, opened in December 2018 and formally inaugurated the following January and February, timed to Bologna's Art City/Arte Fiera calendar (C-FB-07). It operated continuously — confirmed at multiple points across 2019, 2020, 2022, 2023 and into January 2025 — until its closure at approximately the end of September 2025, nearly seven years after opening (OWNER-CONFIRMED). This second venue is not mentioned anywhere in Working Paper v0.2; it belongs squarely in this history and should not be collapsed into L'Altro Spazio S.r.l., the entity that ran the original venue.

In October 2022, the same Pratello SRLS opened a further location: **L'Altro Spazio da Osvaldo**, at Via del Pratello 96/A-B, run under a business-lease arrangement rather than as a separate new company. The organisation's own announcement post, signed jointly by both founders, explicitly recapped the venue lineage — "the first L'Altro Spazio opened in 2015 at Via Nazario Sauro 24/F, and L'Altro Spazio al Pratello, born in 2018" — and stated the 80%-disabled-staff figure discussed in §9 (C-FB-08, C-FB-20). Da Osvaldo was discontinued in 2024, after proving economically unsustainable: costs materially higher than expected, and income insufficient to sustain the location (OWNER-CONFIRMED, at a deliberately high level; §11 explains why no further financial detail is given here). One internal record places this venue's opening a year later than the Facebook archive's own contemporaneous post does; this paper uses 2022, consistent with the Facebook record, as the working chronology, while preserving the discrepancy rather than silently resolving it (C-FB-08).

For at least part of 2022–2023, then, the organisation ran **three physical venues simultaneously** under two distinct legal entities — Via Nazario Sauro under L'Altro Spazio S.r.l., and both Pratello addresses under L'Altro Spazio Pratello SRLS — a fact confirmed by an April 2023 post inviting bookings "at our two locations at 96/a and 29/a" alongside continued Nazario Sauro programming (C-FB-10).

A fourth concept, **JASPACE**, a coworking and creative-workspace idea at Via del Pratello 96/C, was prepared in January 2024: an interest-form for potential users, an intended near-term opening, and a related creative-workshop concept referred to as "Studio Blume." This paper is deliberately precise about what this evidence does and does not support: it shows a genuine, concrete step toward opening — more than an idea on a whiteboard — but not sustained operation, a meaningful user base, or that the concept ever moved past pilot stage. **JASPACE should be described as a proposed, experimental concept whose implementation remains uncertain, not as an established fourth venue** (C-FB-09).

By 2024, the organisation's Pratello-area visibility had dropped sharply — roughly seventy-two mentions of "Nazario Sauro" across the year's posts against seven combined mentions of both Pratello addresses — as da Osvaldo closed, and no evidence reviewed for this paper shows JASPACE progressing beyond its preparatory/pilot stage. Only Pratello 29/A survived into 2025, itself closing that September. By early 2025, the organisation's public footprint had narrowed back to two addresses: Via Nazario Sauro and Parco 11 Settembre (C-FB-11).

Read across this whole section, the pattern is genuinely striking, though it should be stated precisely rather than smoothed into a single continuous story. Parco 11 Settembre predates Jascha's involvement entirely, and Via Polese was opened by Nunzia before Jascha entered the project at all — the practice did not begin with "the same two people" present in every environment from the start. What is accurate, and still notable, is narrower: across these environments, overlapping people, recurring practices and a persistent public identity become visible, and from the 2015 rebuild onward, Jascha and Nunzia become the recurring founders and operators of the shared L'Altro Spazio project specifically — across at least five distinct physical environments and one further concept that never fully launched, spanning four distinct L'Altro Spazio legal entities (Associazione L'Altro Spazio, L'Altro Spazio S.r.l., L'Altro Spazio Pratello SRLS, and, from 2025, Cooperativa L'Altro Spazio), alongside Associazione Farm's separate and overlapping presence at Parco. This paper offers the environments-not-single-venue interpretation as a genuinely useful lens for reading that pattern — but it is careful to state what the interpretation is *not*: it is not a claim that these entities shared one continuous legal identity, and it is not a claim that every environment succeeded or was equally durable or equally accessible. Via Polese closed abruptly after a few months. Da Osvaldo failed as an economic expansion after two years. JASPACE may never have moved past a preparatory stage. Pratello 29/A ran for nearly seven years and closed anyway. Discontinuity, not just continuity, belongs in this history.

## 11. Failure, friction and limits

An organisation that only tells success stories is not being honest about how it actually learned anything, and the evidence gathered for this paper supports a genuine record of things that did not work.

**Via Polese closed abruptly within months of opening**, forced shut by serious water damage under its original association. This is a real, significant discontinuity — the sixteen-month public silence that followed it should be read as exactly what it was: a venue closing, an association ceasing, and two people deciding what to do next — and this paper treats it as institutionally formative: it is the direct cause of the decision, months later, to rebuild the same underlying idea with a new legal structure and a different location (§4–5). But it should not be described as a business failure. Nothing in the evidence establishes that the venue or its underlying concept was economically unsuccessful, or that poor operation caused the closure; what is established is an abrupt, externally forced ending.

**L'Altro Spazio da Osvaldo failed after roughly two years**, discontinued in 2024 because it proved economically unsustainable — costs materially higher than expected, income insufficient to cover them (§10). This paper deliberately does not reproduce any financial, creditor, litigation, tax, banking, or payroll detail behind that characterisation, in line with the confidentiality boundary established for this research (see the note at the end of this section). What can be said, at the level appropriate to an institutional history, is that expanding to a third simultaneous physical location did not work, and that the failed expansion placed substantial strain on L'Altro Spazio Pratello SRLS, the entity that carried it — strain that contributed materially to the contraction of that entity's operations afterward, culminating in Pratello 29/A's own closure roughly a year later (§10). The underlying financial and legal detail behind that strain and contraction remain outside the scope of this paper.

**No evidence reviewed for this paper establishes that JASPACE progressed beyond its preparatory/pilot stage**, and this paper treats that as a live, unresolved uncertainty rather than converting it retroactively into either a success or a failure. An idea prepared with genuine intent — an interest-form, a related workshop concept, an intended opening date — that then disappears from the public record without an announced launch or an announced closure is its own category, distinct from either a venue that opened and later closed or one that was simply abandoned as a concept.

**Cena al Buio's entire 2024 promotional gap** remains genuinely unexplained. The format is present in the record every other year across a full decade, disappears from public posts for the whole of 2024, and returns unchanged for Halloween 2025. This paper does not assume the gap reflects an operational pause rather than simply a year without social-media promotion — the difference matters and cannot be resolved from this evidence alone (§8, C-FB-23).

**The March–September 2025 institutional transition is almost entirely invisible in the organisation's own public voice.** The board's approval of Jascha Blume's employment and the affitto d'azienda arrangement (March 2025), and the Comune's Parco pact (May 2025) — both genuinely significant institutional events — occur during, or immediately before, a roughly three-and-a-half-month total posting silence, from 27 May to 11 September 2025. This paper states clearly what the evidence supports and what it explicitly does not: the silence is real and dateable; **its cause is unknown**, and the owner has confirmed he does not know why it occurred. No inference should be drawn linking it to the SRL-to-cooperative transition, a crisis, a closure, reduced operational activity, or a deliberate communications strategy — none of these is established, and the silence is not evidence for or against any of them (C-FB-14). The only safe general lesson, applicable well beyond this one instance, is methodological: across the entire archive, the organisation's public Facebook voice is remarkably continuous through every documented legal and governance change — the Farm-to-SRL Parco transition, the cooperative's 2022 registration, both Pratello incorporations, and the 2025 transition are all essentially invisible in the account's own posts, with a single exception (a July 2024 post naming "Coop L'Altro Spazio" in a children's-programme credit line, discussed in §13). **Public silence about an institutional change is not evidence that the change did not happen, or did not matter.**

A confidentiality note applies to this entire section and to the paper as a whole: nothing here reproduces, references, or summarises any financial figures, debts, creditors, litigation strategy, banking detail, tax dispute, or private payroll information relating to any entity discussed in this paper, in line with the explicit boundary set for this research. Where a failure or a discontinuation is discussed, only the high-level organisational fact and lesson are given — never the underlying private material.

## 12. Language catching up with practice

The Facebook archive makes it possible, for the first time in this research programme, to trace how the organisation's own public voice has talked about disability, accessibility and inclusion across a full decade — not as a side note, but as its own kind of evidence.

The arc is genuinely interesting and not flattering in a straightforwardly linear way. In 2014, the organisation's own copy uses no disability vocabulary at all — the only such language that year, "sordo-ciechi" (deafblind), comes entirely from an external Swiss press article the account shared, not from its own words. By 2015, the account's own voice adopts concrete, design-specific language tied to a single physical space: "a misura delle persone con disabilità," a venue "built to fit disabled people." Through 2016 and 2017, "disabilità" appears increasingly in the account's own copy, but a large share of that content takes the form of shared, third-party human-interest stories — a blind sculptor, an armless painter, a Deaf pianist — framed in explicitly celebratory, inspirational terms ("a demonstration to the contrary," "it didn't stop him becoming an artist"). This "inspirational disability story" mode sits in genuine tension with the concrete, operationally precise design practice visible in the very same years — subtitled and audio-described public cinema, a Deaf brewer as a genuine creative partner, Braille-captioned exhibitions (`concept-language-evolution.md` §2, §7). Both things were true of the organisation in 2016 and 2017: it was doing precise, unusually thoughtful accessibility work, and it was also, in some of its own public storytelling choices, using framing that would read as dated and uncomfortable by 2026 standards.

**This paper preserves that tension deliberately rather than editing it out.** An organisational history that quietly rewrites its own 2016 language to sound like 2026 is not more accurate; it is less honest. The 2016–17 material is kept exactly as evidenced, not smoothed over, because the discomfort it produces is itself part of the evidence for how the organisation's thinking actually developed rather than arriving fully formed.

By 2018, the register shifts toward explicit employment-rights framing, and by 2023, toward an activist and rights-based vocabulary borrowed from the wider disability-justice movement — participation in Disability Pride Bologna, with hashtags like `#disabilityjustice`, and the term "neurodivergenti" entering the account's own working vocabulary for the first time, attached to a concrete, operational accessibility policy (priority booking and wheelchair-routing directions, April 2023) rather than to a slogan (`concept-language-evolution.md` §2, §7).

By 2024–2025, a different and in some ways opposite problem appears: "#Inclusione" and "#Accessibilità" become close to default hashtags, attached to karaoke nights, DJ sets, vintage markets and pasta dinners regardless of whether that specific event involves any accessibility feature distinct from the venue's general baseline. This is a real, measurable shift from the 2016–18 pattern, in which the same words were reserved for posts describing a genuinely concrete accommodation. **The working interpretation this paper offers — clearly labelled as interpretation, not settled fact — is that practice sometimes ran ahead of the language available to describe it in the organisation's early years, and that later, the language sometimes ran ahead of what any single activity could actually demonstrate.** Two illustrations support taking this seriously without over-claiming it: the 2016–17 inspirational-framing material, discussed above, where concrete practice was arguably more sophisticated than the words used to narrate it; and the 2024–25 blanket-hashtag pattern, where the words may now claim more, more generically, than any one post demonstrates — even as the underlying practice, in the same period, reaches its most institutionally sophisticated point yet, with internationally-funded Deaf/hearing art (§8; `concept-language-evolution.md` §9).

Two specific self-descriptions deserve to be named and handled carefully, because both risk being repeated uncritically in future public communication. In November 2017, the account described itself as *"il primo bar inclusivo d'Italia"* — "the first inclusive bar in Italy" — an unhedged, unsourced claim with no independent substantiation found anywhere in this research. And the October 2022 "80% of staff" figure discussed in §9 remains an entirely self-reported claim. Neither should be repeated as an established fact; both are usable, carefully, as historical evidence of what the organisation said about itself at a specific moment (C-FB-21, C-FB-20).

One quiet, almost accidental line of copy from late 2025 is worth ending this section on, because it captures the propositions' underlying logic more precisely than any policy document in this research does. A routine party flyer for a November 2025 karaoke night reads: *"Sentire e ascoltare non sono la stessa cosa"* — "Hearing and listening are not the same thing." Nobody wrote this as a mission statement. It is the clearest instance anywhere in the archive of the organisation's accumulated thinking surfacing spontaneously, in ordinary commercial copy, rather than in deliberate institutional language (`facebook-archive-chronology.md` 2025).

## 13. From practice to institution

Everything in §2 through §12 describes a practice built through venues, programmes and relationships. This section describes what happened when that practice began, from around 2022 onward, to acquire formal institutional structure — and it is careful to keep the two things (activity, and legal/accounting status) analytically separate, because the evidence shows they do not always move together.

**Cooperativa L'Altro Spazio** was registered in Bologna's cooperative register on 27 June 2022 (DOCUMENTED; S13, S19). This date matters because it rules out a common and inaccurate shorthand: the cooperative has not existed since 2010, and should never be described that way. Its earliest years were quiet in a formal sense — the 2024 financial statements record no operating revenue and describe the cooperative as inactive in accounting terms (S13–S14). Nowhere in the entire 930-post Facebook archive is the cooperative mentioned by name in 2022 or 2023 at all (C-FB-13).

But "inactive" needs a careful qualifier, because it is not the same as "nothing happened." In May 2024, the Comune di Bologna's Quartiere Porto-Saragozza awarded the cooperative an €8,000 contribution, scored 81 points, for a project called "Cultura Verde: concerti, arte e lettura nel Porto Verde di Bologna" (S15). And in July 2024, a Facebook post about a children's workshop programme at Parco 11 Settembre — "Il Porto Ritrovato" — explicitly credits "**Coop L'Altro Spazio**," alongside Associazione Farm and three other partners, as the programme's deliverer. This is the only place in the entire archive where the cooperative is named in the organisation's own public voice, and it independently corroborates that the Cultura Verde-funded project was actually delivered, not merely awarded (C-FB-12). The correct, owner-confirmed reading distinguishes two separate facts that should not be collapsed into one: **the cooperative's 2024 accounts show no operating revenue and describe accounting inactivity; the related project activity was genuinely delivered in 2024; and the associated public financial contribution was received and liquidated in 2025**, after eligible expenditure was reported. Accounting status and project activity are different facts, and 2024 should be described as a year of preparatory institutional activity without ordinary operating revenue — not simply as "inactive."

2025 is when the documentary record becomes dense. On 5 March, the cooperative's board approved a membership change and a full-time, indefinite employment relationship for Jascha Blume as coordinator of socio-educational services, from 1 April (S16). On 24 March, the board authorised an affitto d'azienda — a business-lease arrangement — bringing the Via Nazario operating business, its equipment and authorisations, and its premises lease, across from L'Altro Spazio S.r.l. into the cooperative, explicitly framed as supporting socio-educational expansion and employment continuity (S17). Owner-confirmed history states that, from 2025, both the Via Nazario venue and the Parco strand operate under the cooperative (O01).

On 21 May 2025, the Comune di Bologna and the cooperative signed a 36-month collaboration pact for Parco 11 Settembre — a genuinely different kind of relationship from a project grant. It defines a participatory-management ambition built on sustainability, accessibility and shared civic competencies, alongside cultural, educational, recreational, reading, work-orientation, anti-discrimination, fragility-support and environmental-care activities, and it establishes ongoing monitoring, reporting and safety responsibilities rather than a one-time funding decision (S18). This paper treats the distinction between the 2024 Cultura Verde award and the 2025 Parco pact as institutionally significant: the first is a conventional grant relationship — apply, receive, deliver, report; the second is a shared-administration arrangement in which the organisation takes on ongoing civic responsibility for a public space alongside the municipality, with the possibility of the agreement's own terms being reopened during implementation to evaluate progress and agree corrections (S18). Whether practice matches the pact's ambitions in full is a separate evaluation question this paper does not attempt to answer; the structural shift itself — from grantee to co-administrator — is documented.

By November 2025, the cooperative applied for public-procurement service admission (MePA) across six categories: recreational/cultural/event organisation, social welfare, training, translation and interpretation, bar management, and catering (S19). This is a genuine signal of institutional direction and of an intent to become legible to public purchasers as a multi-service organisation, not only a venue. It is not evidence of delivery volume, revenue, or outcomes in any of those categories — the filing itself records zero comparable-contract figures in the relevant sections, a fact that should be read as a limitation of the filing's own scope at that date, not as proof the organisation had no 2025 activity (C023 in the v0.2 register already makes this explicit, and this paper preserves that caution).

A handful of 2025–2026 project applications and proposals extend the same institutional direction further, and this paper is careful to label each by what it actually establishes rather than what it hopes for. **SEGNI DI PACE**, a formal grant application submitted to Regione Emilia-Romagna in March 2026 with an €18,000 budget and a partner organisation's signed letter of adhesion, proposes bringing LIS-based, Deaf-led encounter work into schools and public services alongside migrant-community collaboration. The application proves it was submitted, with those specific intentions, targets and a named partner; it does not prove the grant was awarded, that the project was implemented as written, that any target number of participants was reached, or that any proposed public-service partner beyond the one signed adhesion actually participated (S22–S23). A December 2025 commercial proposal develops Cena al Buio into a corporate B2B offer with defined formats, pricing and an optional training module — real evidence of an offer that existed at that date, not proof of how often it was actually delivered (S24). Draft 2026 planning documents for Cineporto/Parco discuss sustainability, a social kitchen, training, and potential university and environmental partnerships; named organisations in these drafts remain "planned" or "possible" collaborations unless a separate agreement establishes otherwise (S27). A substantial concept document called Sencity proposes an international, multisensory cultural festival; in the current evidence base, it demonstrates ambition and a distinctive cultural orientation, not that an Italian edition has occurred (S26).

None of this should be read cynically: applying for grants and drafting multi-year proposals is ordinary activity for a cooperative at this stage, not evidence of overreach. The discipline this paper insists on is narrower: a proposal is a proposal until it is delivered, and the organisation's own future communication should say so plainly, because the gap between "we submitted this" and "this happened" is exactly the kind of gap that erodes institutional credibility if it is ever blurred, even by accident.

## 14. Seven propositions after ten years

The seven propositions were formulated in 2026, as a way of reading back over the practice this paper has just described. They are working propositions, not doctrine, and the evidence gathered for this version supports each of them unevenly — some are now grounded in specific, dated, cross-referenced material; others remain closer to an aspiration the organisation is still working to realise. Each is treated below on its own terms.

**Proposition 1 — People are often limited not by themselves, but by systems designed from unspoken assumptions.**
This is the proposition with the clearest and earliest evidence in this entire research programme. Nunzia's own reframing — from "the problem was deafness" to "our spaces had simply been built without them in mind" — is arguably the cleanest single statement of the idea found in any source reviewed, and it predates the formal proposition by over a decade (C-ORIG-04). Maurice's testimony frames handicap relationally, as something appearing between a person and an environment, not a property of the person alone (S01). The 2025 Comune pact extends the same logic to public space, treating accessibility and participation as design conditions for the park rather than exceptional accommodations bolted onto it (S18). **Tension:** the organisation's own 2016–17 public voice sometimes described individual disabled people in exactly the inspirational, individual-overcoming-adversity terms the proposition argues against (§12) — a reminder that holding a systemic insight and still reproducing an older, individual-focused framing in public storytelling are not mutually exclusive, and the organisation has done both.

**Proposition 2 — Translation is not something that happens afterwards; it is one of the ways meaning is created.**
This proposition has the richest and most varied evidence base of any of the seven. Jascha's own lifelong experience of being routed through interpreters — "close to the conversation, but never completely inside it" — is the single strongest personal articulation found anywhere in this research (C-ORIG-06). The 2015 incorporation deed's bilingual structure — a documented language-access and translation procedure following from Jascha's inability to read Italian, with his deafness separately recorded in the same document — is the strongest *formal* evidence: translation operating not in the organisation's programme but in its own founding paperwork (C-FB-26, §5). The archive shows the same idea maturing across a decade — from ordinary sign-language communication behind a bar counter, to a 2023 university/national-institution convening on teaching LIS, to internationally-funded 2025 art treating Deaf sensory experience as compositional material rather than an accommodation (§8). **Tension:** the organisation's own public vocabulary about "autonomia" and "partecipazione" — words closely related to this proposition — is comparatively thin and inconsistent across the decade relative to how consistently the underlying *practice* of communication design appears (§9, §12). The idea is well demonstrated in practice; it is less consistently *named* in the organisation's own words.

**Proposition 3 — A space, process or design communicates before a single word is spoken.**
The Via Nazario construction narrative — spatial prototyping with tape and floor plans, "activity/encounter/social space" language preceding "bar" language, the deliberate gallery-with-bar concept — is the richest evidence for this proposition found anywhere in this research (§6). Independent third-party corroboration exists too: Lonely Planet Italia's 2018 guide independently described Braille menus, a lowered counter and sign-language ordering, without prompting from the organisation itself (`facebook-archive-chronology.md`, 22 Nov 2018). Two later, distinct sources extend the same logic to public space: the organisation's own April 2023 priority-booking and wheelchair-routing policy is a concrete operational rule, not just a design aesthetic (C-FB-19); the 2025 Comune pact, a separate and later instrument, commits to accessibility and participatory management as objectives for Parco, though the pact itself specifies goals and responsibilities rather than the kind of physical wayfinding detail the 2023 policy describes (S18). **Interpretation, not fact:** this proposition connects most directly to the "practice of environments" interpretation offered in §10 — if the proposition is read as being about environments rather than one fixed building, the repeated pattern of building, losing and rebuilding spaces becomes part of the evidence for the proposition, not a complication needing to be explained away. This paper offers that connection as a genuinely useful reading, not a settled conclusion.

**Proposition 4 — Good design reduces the continual adaptation and additional effort demanded from people in order to participate.**
Chiara's beer-tap adaptation and Manuela's account of the bar counter are the clearest small-scale demonstrations (S04–S05). Jascha's own account of the Parco festival — customers taking time, using gesture, "not afraid of hands" — is a case of an environment (in this instance, an informal social one, not a designed physical one) that reduced the compensatory effort normally required of him (C-ORIG-09). The 2023 priority-booking and wheelchair-routing policy is the clearest *operational*, rather than aesthetic, instance: a concrete decision that removes friction rather than merely describing an intention to (C-FB-19). **Limit:** this proposition is easiest to demonstrate at the scale of a single task or a single policy; it is harder to evaluate at the scale of the whole organisation without a current, systematic accessibility audit across physical, sensory, communication, digital and programme access — which this research base does not contain (§16).

**Proposition 5 — Identity emerges not merely through representation but through actual participation.**
The strongest evidence for this proposition is not the construction narrative but the interview corpus's account of ordinary work: people solving problems, being relied upon, disagreeing, and building routines together as colleagues and professionals rather than as represented subjects of someone else's programme (§9, S01–S06). Daniel's testimony sharpens the proposition with an explicit self-determination dimension: participation is not the same as being spoken for or represented by others, and he is critical of hearing people's control over organisations meant to serve Deaf communities (S03). The first build team's participation in Via Nazario's physical construction adds supporting, though weaker, evidence: at least some of the people who would later work there were present during its making (§6) — this shows genuine participation in shaping the environment, but it is evidence of participation, not, by itself, evidence of identity formation, and this paper keeps the two claims distinct. **Tension:** the 2022 "80% of staff" figure is exactly the kind of representational claim this proposition would caution against treating as sufficient on its own — a number about who is present is not, by itself, evidence about what kind of participation, authorship or agency those people actually have (§9, §12).

**Proposition 6 — The most interesting territory is often not the individual person or technology in isolation, but the encounter between them, where possibility and friction emerge.**
Chiara's beer-tap mechanism is the clearest small-scale demonstration in the literal sense the proposition describes: neither Chiara nor the mechanism alone explains what happened; the interesting thing is the specific encounter between a person and a piece of equipment, adjusted until it worked for her (§7, §9, S04). The 2025 Sub_Bar Show is a larger-scale, art-world version of the same idea: touch and subwoofer vibration used as a shared sensory interface between Deaf and hearing participants, where the encounter between bodies and a physical/acoustic technology is the substance of the work, not incidental to it (§8). The RAMP beer collaboration with Deaf brewer Ken Fisher is a further, more informal instance of a product built around a specific person's way of encountering the world, rather than the person or the recipe considered in isolation (§8). **Interpretation:** Jascha's own visual language for the venue — a SpaceX rocket-test poster and a mural depicting "a signal, a deviation in frequency," which he connects personally to his own relationship with sound ("sound never came easily to me") — extends the proposition beyond a strict person/technology pairing into a broader idea of encounter between a person and an environment's material choices (C-ORIG-13, §6); this paper presents that extension explicitly as an interpretive reading, not as the proposition's original scope. The GAZE-plus-TRANSLATION-toward-SPACE/STRUCTURE progression offered in §2–§3 is, in a further and more speculative extension, the same underlying logic applied at the scale of the institution's own origin — two founders' distinct routes into the same underlying problem, meeting and producing something neither route alone would have produced. This paper offers that reading as a hypothesis worth taking seriously, not as an established intellectual history the founders were consciously following at the time.

**Proposition 7 — Accessibility is not an endpoint or checklist, but a way of designing, observing and continuing to learn.**
Nunzia's own account of her early learning process — self-taught, informal, "observed, imitated, made mistakes, and learned" — is a direct, personal demonstration of this proposition as a way of working, not a claim about a finished body of expertise (C-ORIG-01). The repeated, task-level adaptations described throughout the interview corpus — the beer tap, the counter, the informal mutual-aid routines that changed as people changed — are the clearest institutional evidence that accessibility here has actually operated as an iterative practice rather than a one-time design decision (§7, §9). The Comune pact's own provision allowing shared design to be reopened during implementation, to evaluate progress and agree corrections, is the clearest current institutional mechanism embodying this proposition rather than merely asserting it (S18). The improvised, visibly unfinished state of Via Nazario's opening night (§5–§6) is worth keeping as historical texture consistent with this disposition, but this paper does not treat opening before everything was ready as, by itself, proof of an iterative-learning method — an unfinished launch could equally reflect ordinary time pressure. **Limit:** the same evidence that supports this proposition also complicates it — a decade of learning-through-use does not, by itself, guarantee that everything learned has been retained as organisational memory rather than depending on specific people who happened to be present; this is a genuine institutional risk, not resolved by anything in this research, and it is discussed further in §17.

Read together, the seven propositions are not equally proven. Propositions 2 and 3 have the richest, most varied evidence, including formal documentary support unusual for propositions of this kind. Propositions 1, 5 and 7 are well supported experientially but rest more heavily on interpretation and testimony than on independent corroboration. Propositions 4 and 6 are convincingly demonstrated at small scale and remain, honestly, partly hypothetical at the scale of the whole organisation. None of the seven should be presented publicly as proven beyond what this section actually shows.

## 15. What L'Altro Spazio may be

Taken together, the evidence in this paper supports a description more specific than any single label — "bar," "cooperative," "inclusive workplace," "cultural venue" — can carry on its own, and more specific than the working description offered in v0.2.

**L'Altro Spazio is a decade-long practice of building and rebuilding social, cultural and hospitality environments around the people actually in them, carried by two founders whose two starting experiences — gaze and visibility; translation and mediation — converged into a shared practice concerned with space, structure and participation; expressed across at least five physical environments and one further concept that did not fully launch; operated through at least four distinct L'Altro Spazio legal entities, plus Associazione Farm's separate and overlapping history, none of which should be collapsed into one continuous company; and now, since roughly 2022, in the process of turning that accumulated practice into a durable social-cooperative institution with formal public-administration relationships, employment structures and service ambitions.**

This description is deliberately longer and more qualified than a slogan, because the evidence does not support a slogan. It is offered as this paper's working synthesis, not as a finished public description — any public-facing version of this description should be shorter, and should still preserve the distinction between the practice's long history and the cooperative's much shorter legal one.

Two things this description deliberately avoids repeating from earlier framings deserve to be stated directly. It does not claim one continuous legal identity from 2010, or even from 2014, onward — Associazione L'Altro Spazio, L'Altro Spazio S.r.l., L'Altro Spazio Pratello SRLS and Cooperativa L'Altro Spazio are four distinct L'Altro Spazio legal entities, related by people and by public identity, not by uninterrupted legal continuity; Associazione Farm is a further, separate entity with its own overlapping history at Parco, not itself an L'Altro Spazio vehicle. And it does not claim that every environment the organisation has built succeeded, was equally accessible, or was equally durable — Via Polese closed abruptly within months and was not rebuilt under the same association; da Osvaldo failed as an economic expansion; JASPACE may never have moved past a preparatory stage; and even Pratello 29/A, which ran for nearly seven years, eventually closed.

## 16. What remains open

This paper does not resolve, and should not be read as resolving, the following. Some are carried forward unchanged from v0.2; some are new to this version.

- The current legal representative and board composition after March 2026, and the exact relationship between board records that identify different people as legal representative five weeks apart (v0.2 §11.5, C040–C041).
- The current number of workers, current cooperative membership, and current proportion of Deaf, disabled and other workers — none of this is established by anything reviewed for this paper, and the 2022 "80% of staff" figure should not stand in for it.
- A current, systematic accessibility audit across physical, sensory, communication, digital and programme access at both Via Nazario and Parco — this paper repeatedly notes where an accessibility claim is well supported historically but not established as a current fact.
- Whether Cena al Buio's 2024 promotional gap reflects reduced operation or simply reduced social-media activity.
- The exact administrative date of the Parco transition from Associazione Farm toward L'Altro Spazio S.r.l. — narrowed by this research to "not yet public as of September 2017," but not otherwise resolved, and not treated as a blocker.
- The precise reason for the 27 May–11 September 2025 posting silence — explicitly unknown, and not to be speculated on further without new evidence.
- Which 2025–2026 project applications (SEGNI DI PACE, Hub Nazario, the Cineporto/Parco 2026 draft) were actually funded and implemented, and which named partners in those documents became real working relationships rather than remaining proposed.
- Whether JASPACE ever moved beyond its January 2024 pilot stage, and if so, in what form.
- The exact identity and legal-registration dates of Associazione L'Altro Spazio, the entity that ran Via Polese — a non-blocking gap, since its operational history and closure are otherwise well supported.
- Whether the organisation's public accessibility vocabulary from 2024–2025 onward is being deliberately re-anchored to concrete, describable practice, or continuing to drift toward genericism — a live question this paper raises rather than answers (§12).

None of these should delay organisational development, publication of a carefully scoped public history, or further work. They are recorded here as exactly what they are: open questions, not blockers, following the same non-blocking uncertainty protocol v0.2 established.

## 17. Implications for the next phase

A decade of practice and roughly four years of active institutionalisation together suggest several concrete implications for what comes next — for the organisation itself, and specifically for its digital presence, which is the immediate practical reason this research programme exists.

**Preserve entity distinctions in public communication, not just in this paper.** Any future public history, website copy, grant application or partnership document should be able to say, in one or two sentences, which legal entity is responsible for what is being described, without needing a reader to parse five entities' worth of nuance. The alternative — letting "L'Altro Spazio" stand in silently for whichever entity is convenient at the time — is exactly the kind of imprecision this whole research programme has worked to correct.

**Do not let institutional maturity flatten into generic language.** Section 12's central finding — that the organisation's own accessibility vocabulary has, in places, become more generic even as its underlying practice has become more sophisticated — is a direct, practical warning for any future public voice, including a rebuilt website. The organisation's most distinctive material is its *specific* practice: a bilingual incorporation deed, a decade-long dark-dining format that never changed its core mechanism, an internationally-funded Deaf/hearing art residency. None of that needs a blanket hashtag to be compelling; it needs to be told precisely.

**Preserve the record of failure and discontinuity as institutional knowledge, not as things to be quietly omitted.** Via Polese's abrupt closure, da Osvaldo's economic failure, and JASPACE's uncertain fate are not embarrassments to be minimised in future communication. They are evidence that the organisation has actually learned to distinguish a viable environment from an unsustainable one — a claim that is only credible if the discontinuities are visible alongside the successes.

**Treat the interview corpus and origin narratives as living oral history, not as source material to be mined once and filed away.** Both bodies of material are explicitly described, by the people who produced them, as prepared for eventual public storytelling use. This paper has used them as evidence; a future stage of this work should consider how to use them, carefully and with consent, as the organisation's own voice in public communication — distinct from, and more textured than, marketing copy.

**Build the next accessibility claim from an actual current audit, not from historical evidence alone.** Nearly every strong accessibility claim in this paper is historical or point-in-time. Before any future public communication makes a present-tense accessibility claim about Via Nazario or Parco, that claim should be checked against current physical, sensory, communication, digital and programme conditions — separately, per the distinction v0.2 already established and this paper reaffirms.

**Treat the 2022–2026 institutionalisation phase as an experiment still in progress, and evaluate it as one.** The documentary record shows increasing formalisation across governance, employment, public partnership and service positioning (§13). An interpretive risk identified in this research — not a conclusion stated by the board records themselves — is that formalisation could either preserve what made the practice work or flatten it into generic procedure. The next phase of this research programme, and of the organisation's own self-reflection, should watch specifically for whether the cooperative's growing service ambitions (MePA categories, training offers, corporate Cena al Buio packages) are extending the underlying method into new contexts, or diluting it into a more generic version of itself.

**Keep the two-track history and the non-blocking uncertainty protocol as permanent working tools, not as artefacts of this one research pass.** New archives, new interviews and new formal records will keep arriving. The discipline this paper and its predecessor have built — distinguishing what happened from what people remember from what official records establish from what was proposed but not demonstrated — is the organisation's actual intellectual property at this point, arguably as much as any single programme or venue. It should outlast this specific document.
